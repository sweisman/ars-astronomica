A vast attempt to explain the whole hidden world beneath our feet. Drawing on a
daring descent into the smoking crater of Vesuvius and a lifetime of reading, it
describes a planet riddled with fiery channels and underground rivers, ranging
across volcanoes, springs, tides, metals, minerals, fossils, poisons, subterranean
creatures, and the secrets of alchemy.

## What is in this book

The work is *in XII libros digestus* — set out in twelve books — and it begins
further back than its title suggests. The first book is *Centrographicus*, on the
centre: a sequence of definitions, canons and corollaries on centres of gravity
and of magnitude, establishing the geometry of a body's centre before Kircher
descends into the particular body that is the Earth. From that foundation the
books move outward through the planet's fires and waters to its metals, minerals,
fossils, poisons and subterranean creatures, and finally to alchemy and the
transmutation of substances.

The book is famous for its engravings, above all the cross-section of a globe
threaded with fire and burning at its core — one of the most reproduced images
in the history of the earth sciences, and the picture most people carry of what
the seventeenth century thought the underground looked like.

## Why it matters

*Mundus Subterraneus* is the foundational synthesis of early modern earth
science, and it is valuable precisely because it does not sort cleanly into what
later ages kept and what they discarded. The volcanic observation is first-hand
and hard-won; the hydrology is a serious attempt to account for springs, tides
and the circulation of water through the Earth; and both sit alongside Hermetic
and biblical speculation without apology. Reading it is the most direct way to see
how a learned seventeenth-century mind actually held those things together,
rather than how later summaries have separated them.

## About this edition

The translated source is the 1665 printing. Its front matter records the standing
of a work of this scale in Counter-Reformation Europe: approbations from the
author's order, an engraved portrait of and dedicatory epistle to Pope Alexander
VII, and printing privileges granted by both the Holy Roman Emperor and
Charles II of England.
