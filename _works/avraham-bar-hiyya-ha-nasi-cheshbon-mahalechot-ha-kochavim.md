A medieval Hebrew treatise on mathematical astronomy and the computation of
the Jewish calendar. It sets out the structure of the heavens and the courses of
the sun, moon, and planets, then applies them to the molad (conjunction), the
tekufot (seasonal points), the nineteen-year cycle, and the rules of intercalation.

## What is in this book

The work is organized into twenty gates, and it begins further back than its title
suggests: the first gate teaches arithmetic itself — subtracting one number from
another, multiplying degrees and fractions, dividing them, extracting roots. Bar
Ḥiyya assumes a reader who wants the astronomy but may not yet have the
computation, and supplies both.

The second gate turns to arcs and their chords, the trigonometric foundation of
everything that follows, and the next gates work through the geometry of the
sphere: the arc between the surfaces, the ascensions of the zodiacal signs on the
equator and on the dividing circle, the latitudes of places north of the equator.

The later gates are where the book's cosmopolitanism shows. Alongside the
length of the solar and lunar years, bar Ḥiyya gives procedures for converting
Hebrew years to the Egyptian reckoning, sets out the basis of the reckoning of the
Arabs, converts between Anno Mundi and Hijra years in both directions, finds the
weekday on which a Hijra year begins, computes planetary motions from Hijra
years, and gives the root of the reckoning of the Greeks and Romans by the Sun.

## Why it matters

Bar Ḥiyya stands at the head of the Hebrew tradition of computational astronomy
that later authors such as Gersonides would extend — he is writing this material
in Hebrew, with its vocabulary, more or less for the first time. The era-conversion
gates capture his position precisely: a scholar in twelfth-century Barcelona,
known in Latin as Savasorda, moving between Jewish, Muslim and classical
reckonings because his world contained all three.
