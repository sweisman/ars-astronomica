The earliest systematic Hebrew treatise on the science of the calendar, written in
early-twelfth-century Barcelona. It sets out the astronomical and mathematical
foundations of the Jewish calendar — the calculation of the new moon, the cycles
that govern leap years, the reckoning of the seasons, and the interplay of lunar
and solar time.

## What is in this book

The work is built in discourses (*ma'amarim*), each divided into gates. The first
discourse establishes the physical picture before the arithmetic: the form of the
firmament, the motion of the sphere over the earth and how the constellations
stand within it, and then the variation among places of habitation from north to
south — the fact that makes calendar reckoning depend on where the observer is.
Ten gates carry that argument through.

The second discourse turns to computation proper, and to the machinery a
practitioner needs. Its later gates work through deriving the *moladot* from
tables built for intercalation, supply the tables of the moladot in the simple and
double cycles, and — characteristically — devote a part to checking a molad, to
determine whether an error has crept into the reckoning. Bar Ḥiyya is as
concerned with verifying a result as with obtaining it.

## Why it matters

This is where the Hebrew science of the calendar becomes a systematic discipline
rather than a body of inherited rules. Bar Ḥiyya writes it in Hebrew, supplies both
the astronomy and the arithmetic, and organizes it so it can be taught — and later
calendar scholars copied that organization for centuries. It is the foundation on
which the rest of the Hebrew calendrical tradition in this corpus stands.

## About this edition

The printed volume carries an editor's apparatus alongside the text: an
introduction, a life of the author surveying his other works — *Tsurat ha-Arets*,
*Cheshbon Mahalechot ha-Kochavim*, the *Luchot ha-Nasi* tables, *Hegyon
ha-Nefesh* and *Megillat ha-Megalleh* — and explanatory notes, including the
editor's own attempt at a difficult dictum in the ninth gate of the second
discourse, addressed to bar Ḥiyya directly.
