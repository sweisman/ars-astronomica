A foundational work of medieval cosmology that lays out the shape of the cosmos
as the Greeks and their Arabic heirs understood it: a spherical earth at the center
of nested celestial spheres, the paths of sun and moon, and the zones and
geography of the inhabited world.

## What is in this book

The book proceeds by gates, each opened by a series of *petaḥim* — "openings" —
which give it the feel of a structured course rather than a treatise. The first gate
sets out the structure of the heavens and the earth, and works through to the
inhabited part of the earth: which portion of a spherical world is habitable, and
how it is divided.

The second gate carries the scheme further, and the third turns to the Moon —
the clarification of its motion, including the compound lunar motion that any
serious account of the calendar or of eclipses depends on. The fourth gate treats
eclipses of the two luminaries directly.

## Why it matters

Its importance is as much linguistic as astronomical: this is one of the first
scientific works written in Hebrew rather than Arabic, at a moment when a Jewish
scholar wanting this material would ordinarily have read it in Arabic. Bar Ḥiyya
had to make Hebrew carry it — the vocabulary, the geometry, the whole
apparatus — and in doing so opened the tradition that the rest of this corpus's
Hebrew astronomy belongs to.

## About this edition

The translation is made from the richly illustrated first printed edition (Basel,
1546), which carries Latin notes by the Hebraist Sebastian Münster. That volume
is bilingual — each work printed once in Hebrew, then again in Latin with the
apparatus of Münster and Erasmus Oswald Schreckenfuchs — and only the
Hebrew portion was translated, with Münster's facing annotations used as a
parallel witness to resolve the difficult sixteenth-century type. The edition
includes its own note on how it was produced.
