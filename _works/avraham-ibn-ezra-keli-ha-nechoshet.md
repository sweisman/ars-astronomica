The earliest surviving Hebrew treatise on the astrolabe, composed in the
mid-twelfth century. It explains how to build the instrument and how to use it to
tell time, determine latitude, locate the stars, and cast astrological judgments.

## What is in this book

The work runs to some thirty-four gates, and reads as a graded course in what
one instrument can be made to do. The first names the parts of the astrolabe and
the markings upon it. The next gates take the altitude of the sun by day and the
stars by night, find the sun's true place from the rete, and locate the point
opposite the sun.

Then comes a long, patient sequence on time. Ibn Ezra distinguishes seasonal
("crooked") hours from equal hours and works out how to obtain each from the
instrument by day and by night, how many of each the day or night contains, and
how to convert between the two systems in either direction — the practical
problem of a world where two incompatible reckonings of the hour were both in
use.

Later gates handle declinations, risings and settings, the equinoctial point, the
day-arc and night-arc, and the breadth of a city — that is, its latitude. A catalogue
of the great fixed stars supplies the coordinates and Arabic-derived names needed
to set the instrument, followed by gates on where a star will rise and set. The
book ends on a practical note that has nothing to do with the heavens: measuring
the height of a tower or tree, and the depth of a thing below you.

## Why it matters

The astrolabe was the period's general-purpose scientific instrument, and this is
the first Hebrew account of it — ibn Ezra writing for readers who had no such text
in their own language. Remarkably, the treatise is no longer the only witness: an
astrolabe bearing Hebrew markings that follow its instructions came to light in
Verona in 2024, the very city where the work was composed.
