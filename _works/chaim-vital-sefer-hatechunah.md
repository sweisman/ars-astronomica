R. Chaim Vital (1542–1620) is remembered as the foremost disciple of R. Isaac
Luria and the man through whom Lurianic Kabbalah reached the world. *Sefer
HaTechunah* is the other side of that mind: a treatise on the astronomy of the
spheres, worked out in the ordinary technical vocabulary of the science.

## What is in this book

The book treats the standard subjects of pre-telescopic mathematical astronomy —
the sphere and its circles, the motions of the sun and moon, the order and
periods of the planets, and the computations by which positions in the heavens
are found.

Vital's own preface promises four parts: the premises of the science, the making
of the tables, the manner of making them, and the molads, tekufot and fixings
derived from those tables. What the manuscript delivers is a complete first
part, not a complete book — the later parts were to be tabular and drawn from an
earlier astronomer rather than computed afresh.

## Why it matters

The division between kabbalist and astronomer is one the period did not
recognize as sharply as later readers have. Vital's cosmology of emanation and
his cosmology of spheres belong to one person and one century, and the technical
astronomy was not a youthful diversion he outgrew.

For the corpus, the book stands with the other Hebrew astronomical texts —
Gersonides, Yesod Olam, Nechmad VeNa'im — as evidence of how thoroughly the
science of the heavens was pursued inside the Jewish learned tradition.

## About this edition

This is a critical edition, though not in the strict academic sense of a
recension drawn from every surviving witness.

The base text is Chester Beatty Ms. Heb. 762, written in a rabbinic hand in
Damascus in the early seventeenth century — the city where Vital spent his last
two decades, within a generation of his death, and the earliest known witness to
the text. Its seventeen astronomical diagrams are executed in gold, red and
blue; two eighteenth-century Damascus kabbalists signed the opening leaf.

**Every reading in the body is the manuscript's.** Collated into the apparatus is
the printed tradition — a modern resetting of the first printing (Jerusalem,
1866) — together with the notes its own editors and annotators left at hard
places. The print has nowhere been allowed to alter the manuscript's text: where
the two differ, both readings stand.

This work is **citation grade**: read complete against the manuscript rather
than sampled.
