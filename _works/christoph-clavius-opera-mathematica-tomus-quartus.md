Tomus IV is Clavius's *Gnomonices* — the most exhaustive Renaissance treatise on
gnomonics, the science of sundials and shadow-casting.

## What is in this volume

Across eight books it develops the geometry of the gnomon's shadow and shows
how to lay out sundials of every kind: horizontal, vertical, inclined, on any plane
and for any latitude. The first book begins where a reader would expect a
technical manual not to — with timepieces in general, their necessity and their
invention — before turning to sciotheric dials and their various kinds.

What follows is the underlying spherical-astronomical theory of hour-lines,
carried through in construction diagrams and in tables of shadow lengths and
hour-line positions. The volume closes with a description of an instrument for
drawing the lines mechanically, so that a dial could be laid out without
recomputing its geometry from scratch.

## Why it matters

Sundials were the period's public clocks, and putting one on an arbitrary wall
facing an arbitrary direction at an arbitrary latitude is a genuinely hard problem
in spherical astronomy. That is what makes the *Gnomonices* more than a
craftsman's manual: it is applied celestial geometry, worked out to a degree of
completeness no one had attempted, by the man who had just reformed the
calendar. The same command of the Sun's apparent motion underlies both
projects.

## About this edition

The translated source is the Mainz printing of 1612, the year of Clavius's death,
dedicated like the other volumes to Prince Johann Gottfried.
