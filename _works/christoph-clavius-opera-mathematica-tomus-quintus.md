Tomus V is Clavius's definitive account of the Gregorian calendar reform, which
he principally engineered — the fullest primary exposition of the calendar the
modern world still uses, written by the man most responsible for it.

## What is in this volume

The *Explicatio Romani Calendarii* expounds the reformed calendar in full: the
correction of the solar year and the equinox, the epacts and golden numbers, the
lunar cycle, and the reckoning of Easter, together with the great computus tables
that put the scheme into daily practice. A compendium of the new method of
restoring the calendar precedes the detail, and the perpetual Gregorian calendar
follows it.

The volume then turns combative. The *Apologia* is Clavius's two-book defence of
the reform against Michael Maestlin's attacks, and an appendix answers further
critics — Joseph Scaliger, Georg Germanus, and François Viète. The dedications
record what was at stake institutionally: the exposition is addressed to Pope
Clement VIII, with a papal document *ad perpetuam rei memoriam*, while the
volume as a whole is dedicated like its fellows to Prince Johann Gottfried.

## Why it matters

The Gregorian calendar is the most widely adopted technical standard ever
devised, and this is its primary source. What the volume preserves that later
accounts lose is that the reform was fiercely contested — by Protestant
astronomers who would not take a calendar from Rome, and by mathematicians
of the first rank who disputed the method itself. Clavius answers them by
argument rather than authority, which makes the *Apologia* a rare thing: a
technical defence of a standard, written while its adoption was still in doubt, by
the person who had to make the case.

## About this edition

The translated source is the Mainz printing of 1612, the year of Clavius's death.
