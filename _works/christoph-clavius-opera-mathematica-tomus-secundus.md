Tomus II collects Clavius's practical mathematics: the mathematics meant to be
used rather than contemplated, and the volume closest to what a student in a
Jesuit college would actually have been drilled in.

## What is in this volume

The *Geometria Practica* treats measurement and surveying — areas, volumes,
heights and distances — and it is as much about instruments as about geometry.
Its first book opens with the parts of the instrument, their construction and use,
and quickly turns into a working table of the quadrant, running division by
division across dozens of printed pages so that a surveyor could read off what he
needed. The prose then returns for problems of rectilinear triangles, right-angled
and oblique, followed by a second book on measuring straight lines with the
astronomical quadrant.

The *Arithmetica Practica* is a full course in computation: the operations,
fractions, proportion, the rule of three, roots, worked through many numeric
examples. The *Algebra* presents the cossist symbolic method — the
*res*, *census* and *cubus* unknowns — for solving equations.

## Why it matters

The Jesuit colleges were among the most systematic mathematical educators in
Europe, and this volume is the curriculum they used. Its character is worth
noticing: page after page of tables, instrument diagrams and worked examples,
aimed at someone who had to measure a field, compute an interest, or lay out a
building. Set beside the Euclid of Tomus I, it shows Clavius deliberately covering
both the demonstrative and the practical faces of mathematics, and treating the
second as no less deserving of a proper textbook.
