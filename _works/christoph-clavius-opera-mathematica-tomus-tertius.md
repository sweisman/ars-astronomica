Tomus III holds Clavius's astronomy. The first and larger part is his commentary
on Sacrobosco's *De sphaera* — the most influential Renaissance astronomy
textbook — reprinting Sacrobosco's brief text and surrounding it with a vast
commentary on spherical astronomy.

## What is in this volume

Clavius opens with an argument for the usefulness of astronomy, then follows
Sacrobosco chapter by chapter, each short passage answered by a commentary
many times its length. The division of the sphere of the world comes first, then
the circles of the sphere, the number and order of the elements, and the count
and motions of the celestial spheres.

The commentary is at its most interesting where it stops expounding and starts
arguing from evidence. Clavius sets out how it was determined that the heavens
move from east to west; by what reasoning it was concluded that there is also a
motion from west to east; how the lower heavens were observed to move about
different poles; and on account of which phenomena astronomers attributed the
motion of trepidation to the fixed stars. From there the work proceeds to risings
and settings, the climes, the motions of the Sun and planets, eclipses, and the
computus.

The second part, the *Astrolabium*, is a comprehensive treatise on the
construction and use of the astrolabe, with its projection geometry and tables.
Both parts are richly illustrated with woodcut diagrams.

## Why it matters

Sacrobosco's *Sphere* was the book through which most of educated Europe first
met the structure of the cosmos, and for more than a century Clavius's edition
was how the *Sphere* itself was read. That makes this volume the best single
record of what mainstream astronomy taught, and how it justified itself, in the
decades on either side of 1600 — the settled position against which Tycho,
Kepler and Galileo were arguing.
