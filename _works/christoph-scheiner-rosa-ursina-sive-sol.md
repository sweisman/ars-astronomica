*Rosa Ursina* is the first comprehensive treatise on sunspots and a foundational
work of telescopic solar astronomy. Scheiner marshals years of observations to
demonstrate that the Sun rotates on its axis — with a period of about a month
and the spots lying on or just above its surface — and establishes the inclination
of the solar axis to the ecliptic.

## What is in this book

The work runs to four books and combines three quite different kinds of writing.
There are the observational records, with engraved plates of spot configurations
tracked across the disc from day to day, which is the evidence the rotation
argument rests on. There is a practical discussion of instruments and of safe
solar-projection methods — how to observe the Sun without destroying one's
eyes, a problem Scheiner had every reason to take seriously. And there is a long
polemic on the history and priority of the sunspot discovery.

The polemic dominates the opening. Book One begins with the occasion of
writing at Rome and proceeds chapter by chapter against Galileo, argued around
the pseudonym "Apelles" under which Scheiner had first published: that Apelles
did not take his doctrine of the spots from his censor, that he did not claim to be
the first discoverer after the censor, with the antitheses of censor and Apelles set
out point against point. The reading layout is dense — two columns, heavy
marginal and interlinear glossing, interleaved with full-page plates.

## Why it matters

The rotation of the Sun and the tilt of its axis are established here, and
established well: Scheiner's spot observations are careful enough that later
astronomers used them. The priority quarrel with Galileo, tedious as it reads, is
part of the historical value — it preserves in detail how a scientific dispute was
actually conducted in the 1620s, by whom, and on what grounds. The book
remained the standard reference on solar phenomena for over a century.

## About this edition

The translated source is the 1630 printing, dedicated to Paolo Giordano II Orsini,
Duke of Bracciano — whose family emblem, the rose and the bear, supplies the
book its otherwise puzzling title.
