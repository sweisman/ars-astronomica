A Hebrew historical chronicle in two parts by David Gans: the first traces Jewish
history from creation to the sixteenth century — biblical figures, sages, and
rabbis; the second surveys world and gentile history from Julius Caesar to
Emperor Rudolph.

## What is in this book

Part One runs by millennia of the Hebrew calendar — first, second, third, fourth,
fifth, sixth — and within them by short dated entries. Its spine is the chain of
transmission: the generations of Tannaim reckoned from the destruction of the
Temple, then the orders of the Amoraim, the Savoraim and the Geonim, carried
forward through the medieval rabbinate. It closes with an index to its own
entries, which is how the book was meant to be used: as a reference for placing a
name in a generation.

Part Two turns to the wider world, from Julius Caesar to the reigning Emperor
Rudolph. Its preface — a defense of the value of studying secular history — is the
passage the book is best known for, and an early and unusually direct statement
of Jewish openness to knowledge from outside the tradition.

## Why it matters

*Tzemach David* was the first history book printed in Hebrew, at Prague in 1592,
and it became a widely read reference well beyond its author's circle. Its two-part
structure is itself the argument: by giving gentile history its own volume rather
than folding it into the margins of Jewish chronology, Gans treated the wider past
as a subject a Jewish reader could legitimately study — the same instinct that took
him to Tycho Brahe's observatory.

## About this edition

The translated volume is the Warsaw 1871 printing of the expanded recension,
which is considerably more than the 1592 book: it carries the Part III
continuation down to 1692, together with later supplements running as far as
1871. The chronicle a nineteenth-century reader held had been extended by three
centuries of other hands.
