David Origanus (David Tost, 1558–1628) taught Greek and mathematics at the
Viadrina University in Frankfurt an der Oder. His *Ephemerides Brandenburgicae*
gives the day-by-day positions of the Sun, Moon and planets for the sixty years
from 1595 to 1655. The positions of the two great lights are computed twice, on
the Tychonic and on the Copernican basis.

## What is in this book

The book opens with its dedication to Johann Sigismund, Elector of Brandenburg,
and with the imperial and French royal privileges that protected it from piracy.
Then comes Origanus's long introduction, which is a textbook in its own right.

Its first part, *On Time*, covers calendars and chronology: the old and the
Gregorian calendars and the cycle of epacts, a table aligning the kings of Judah
and Israel, the length of the day at different latitudes, the equation of time,
and a catalogue of places for converting the ephemerides to other parts of the
earth. The second part, *On Motions*, treats the stars, the orbs and the aspects,
the motion of the heavens, and how to compute a planet's true position for any
time and place. The ephemerides themselves follow, with their supporting tables.

## Why it matters

Ephemerides were the working tools of early modern astronomy, used by
astronomers, physicians, calendar-makers and astrologers alike. Setting
Tycho's parameters beside Copernicus's in a single work let the reader see where
the two systems actually diverged in prediction, at a time when that question was
still open.
