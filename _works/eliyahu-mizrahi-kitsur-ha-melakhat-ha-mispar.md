*Kitsur ha-Melakhat ha-Mispar* ("Compendium of the Art of Number") is a Hebrew
arithmetic textbook by Rabbi Eliyahu Mizrahi (ca. 1455–1526), the chief rabbi of
the Ottoman Empire and one of the foremost Jewish mathematicians of his age.

## What is in this book

Drawing on the Greek and Arabic traditions, it lays out the practical art of
calculation step by step. It opens on the kinds of numeration, then takes the
operations in turn: gathering (addition), striking (multiplication) — including the
methods for multiplying multi-digit numbers — subtraction, and division, each
carried through worked examples. From there it proceeds to the kinds of
fractions, and onward to ratios and proportions, progressions and roots, and the
rules for solving the everyday problems of trade and measurement.

A feature worth noticing is how much attention goes to verification. Each
operation is followed by its check — the scales or balance, the casting-out
method — so the student learns not only how to compute but how to know
whether the computation is right. That instinct runs through the Hebrew
mathematical tradition in this corpus, from bar Ḥiyya's gate on testing a molad
onward.

## Why it matters

Mizrahi wrote from Constantinople at the head of Ottoman Jewry, and this book
became the standard introduction to arithmetic for Hebrew readers. It is lucid and
systematic in a way textbooks of its period often are not, and it is the
computational floor beneath the astronomy: the tables and reckonings elsewhere
in this corpus assume exactly the skills taught here.

## About this edition

The translation is made from the Hebrew text as printed in the first edition
(Basel, 1546), where the work was bound together with bar Ḥiyya's astronomy —
the same volume, with the same Münster apparatus, that supplied *Sefer Tsurat
ha-Arets*. It opens with Münster's address to the studious and fair-minded reader.
