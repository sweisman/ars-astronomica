A mature reckoning with the heavens as the telescope was revealing them — a
sequel to the vast *Almagestum Novum* (1651), built on further years of
observation at Bologna with the collaborator Grimaldi, with fresh measurements
and corrected parameters at center stage.

## What is in this book

The work runs to ten books across two tomes, opening with prolegomena that
argue the ground before any observation is reported: a series of articles on the
fixed stars, on the Sun, and — in an epilogue on the system of the world —
on the arrangement of the cosmos itself.

The books then take the heavens in turn. The first establishes the observations of
the Sun and the foundations of the solar hypotheses; the rest treat eclipses, the
Moon, the fixed stars, and each planet in its own right, before reducing the whole
to working tables a practising astronomer could compute from.

Three strands make it a distinctive record of its moment. It carries some of the
earliest sustained telescopic accounts of Saturn's changing shape, written before
anyone understood that the changes were a ring seen at varying angles. It
describes the bands and spots crossing Jupiter. And it measures the apparent
sizes of the fixed stars — then turns that measurement into an argument, since
stars of such apparent size, placed as far away as a moving Earth requires, would
have to be implausibly vast.

## Why it matters

Riccioli is the most careful of the astronomers who concluded against the Earth's
motion, and this book shows why that position was defensible on the evidence
available. The star-size argument was a real difficulty for Copernicanism, not an
evasion of it — it dissolved only once telescopic optics were understood well
enough to show the measured discs were artifacts. Read now, *Astronomia
Reformata* is a vivid window onto astronomy at the dawn of the telescopic age,
and onto what it was like to reason well from measurements that were quietly
misleading.

## About this edition

The translated source is the 1665 printing in two tomes, which opens with a
dedicatory title page and epistle and supplies two separate indexes — one of the
books, and a fuller one of books and chapters following the prolegomena.
