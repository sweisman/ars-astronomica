Helisaeus Roeslin (Röslin, 1545–1616) was a physician and astrologer who practised
at Haguenau in Alsace. *De opere Dei creationis seu de mundo hypotheses*, "On God's
Work of Creation, or On the World," sets out in numbered theses a complete account
of the created world.

## What is in this book

The theses run from the principles of creation through the elements and the heavens,
and give medicine, alchemy, astronomy and astrology each its place within one
philosophy. The book opens with a "Pythagorean seal of the world" and verses addressed
to the author.

An appendix, written to explain the theses on the system of the world, sets the rival
systems side by side: those of Ptolemy, Copernicus, Nicolaus Raimarus Ursus and Tycho
Brahe, and Roeslin's own. For each he lists the hypotheses and then their consequences,
and tests the consequences against physical, optical and mathematical principles:
whether the planets' orbs would have to pass through one another, whether a planet's
apparent size fits the size of its orb, and whether calculation agrees with
observation. In his own system the Earth rests at the centre, the Sun and Moon circle
the Earth, and the other five planets circle the Sun. He says he had already set it
down when Tycho's book on the comet of 1577 reached him. In the original each system
is shown in a full-page diagram.

## Why it matters

The book appeared in 1597, at the height of the quarrel between Tycho and Ursus over
who had devised the geo-heliocentric system, and it became part of that dispute. It
shows how the competing systems were weighed against one another before the telescope,
by a writer who claimed a system of his own.
