Isaac ibn al-Aḥdab's *Keli Ḥemda* ("The Precious Instrument") — a description of
the construction and use of an equatorium, an instrument for computing
planetary positions geometrically.

## What is in this text

The treatise divides cleanly in two, and the division is the point. The first part
concerns the craft of making the instrument, proceeding by gates through the
work of construction. The second is a separate treatise on its use, opening with
definitions of the instrument's parts and continuing through seven gates of
operation. A standalone ruled astronomical table follows at the end of the
witness, its argument column headed *mispar kelali*.

An equatorium answers a specific difficulty. Computing a planet's position from
tables means a long chain of arithmetic — mean motions, equations, corrections —
and every step invites error. An equatorium replaces that chain with a physical
model: set the instrument for the date, and read the position off directly. It trades
arithmetical labour for geometrical construction, which is why the treatise has to
teach both making and using.

## Why it matters

This is one of a set of medieval Hebrew instrument treatises preserved together —
alongside the quadrant *Roba' Yisrael*, the astrolabe *Keli ha-Neḥoshet*, and ibn
Verga's horizon instrument, all in the same codex. Read together they show a
tradition thinking hard about how to get astronomical results without endless
computation, each instrument attacking a different part of the problem. Ibn
al-Aḥdab, who moved from Castile to Sicily, is also the author of the widely
copied tables *Orah Selulah* — a reminder that the table-maker and the
instrument-maker were often the same person, pursuing the same end by two
routes.

## About this edition

The translated witness is BnF Hébreu 1031, folios 208r–215v — a richly rubricated
Hebrew astronomy anthology that also contains a Hebrew *De Sphaera* and works
of ibn Ezra.
