Jacob ben Machir ibn Tibbon's treatise on the *quadrans novus* — the improved
astronomical quadrant of his own invention — describing how the instrument is
constructed and used for astronomical and time-keeping measurements.

## What is in this text

The work is laid out in sixteen gates (*she'arim*), opening with a table of contents
that names them, and it teaches by worked example as much as by rule: the
folios carry the instrument's use for the rising of signs in the oblique sphere as
against the right sphere, each followed by examples computed through. A table of
fixed stars supplies the coordinates needed to lay out the instrument itself.

The *quadrans novus* was a genuine improvement rather than a variant. A
quadrant is a quarter-circle of brass or wood — cheaper, sturdier and simpler than
an astrolabe — and ibn Tibbon's redesign extended what could be read off one,
bringing much of the astrolabe's functionality onto an instrument that was easier
to make and to carry.

## Why it matters

This is one of the most influential medieval works on astronomical instruments,
and unusually its influence ran outward from Hebrew rather than into it: the
treatise passed into Latin, where its author was known as Profatius Judaeus, and
was cited by Renaissance astronomers. Ibn Tibbon, working at Montpellier and
Marseille, belonged to the family that had made its name translating Arabic
science into Hebrew — and here the traffic reverses, with a Hebrew author's own
invention entering the Latin mainstream.

## About this edition

The translated witness is BnF Hébreu 1031, folios 131r–147v — the same richly
rubricated Hebrew astronomy anthology that carries ibn al-Aḥdab's *Keli Ḥemda*,
a Hebrew *De Sphaera*, and works of ibn Ezra. The manuscript copy is later than
the composition of around 1288.
