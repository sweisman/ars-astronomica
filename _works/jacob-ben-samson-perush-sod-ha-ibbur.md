An early Ashkenazi commentary on the "secret of the calendar" (*sod ha-ibbur*) —
the computation of the Hebrew calendar: the molad (lunar conjunction), the
tequfot (seasonal points), the nineteen-year intercalation cycle, and the rules
governing leap years and festival dates.

## What is in this text

The calendar is the subject, and the calendar in Jewish practice is an
astronomical problem with legal consequences: the molad must be reckoned, the
seasons tracked, the nineteen-year cycle of intercalation applied, and from those
computations the dates of the festivals follow. The commentary works through
that apparatus for a reader who needs to understand the reasoning rather than
merely apply a rule.

Its manuscript context is part of the text's character. The commentary does not
stand alone but sits within a miscellany, and the surrounding leaves carry
material of a quite different kind — a paytanic composition in numbered
strophes, an admonition-poem, the text of the mezuzah with a scribal note on
tefillin, blessings on seeing, the Wayfarer's Prayer. The calendar science is
embedded in a devotional compilation rather than isolated as a technical treatise.

## Why it matters

Calendar computation was one of the few domains where medieval Ashkenazi
scholarship engaged sustained mathematical astronomy, because the liturgical
year could not be kept without it. This commentary is an early witness to how
that knowledge was taught in northern France in the generation around Rashi —
close to the world of the great Talmudic commentators, and showing what
astronomy looked like when it was pursued inside it.

## About this edition

The attribution is to Jacob bar Samson, an early-twelfth-century French scholar
of Rashi's school and an early Ashkenazi authority on the calendar; the corpus
entry marks it *attrib.* accordingly. The text survives within the North French
Hebrew Miscellany (British Library Add MS 11639), copied in northern France in
the late thirteenth century — so the manuscript translated here is roughly a
century and a half later than the composition, and the translation follows folios
511r–545v of that codex.
