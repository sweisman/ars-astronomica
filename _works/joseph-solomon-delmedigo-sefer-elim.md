A wide-ranging Hebrew scientific compendium, framed as a reply to questions put
by the Karaite scholar Zerah ben Nathan. It ranges across mathematics,
astronomy, physics, and metaphysics, and is among the most ambitious Hebrew
scientific books of its age.

## What is in this book

The structure is the frame: twelve major questions — the "springs" of Elim, after
the twelve springs at the biblical Elim — and seventy minor ones, answering the
seventy palms of the same verse, followed by appended treatises. The
question-and-answer form is not decoration. Zerah ben Nathan was a real
correspondent, and the book takes its shape from what he actually wanted to
know.

The content is dense with geometric demonstrations, worked numeric problems,
and astronomical and trigonometric tables. Two things make it notable in Hebrew
letters: it is an early Hebrew witness to logarithms, then barely a decade old in
any language, and it engages post-Copernican astronomy directly rather than at
second hand — Delmedigo had studied at Padua, where he attended Galileo.

## Why it matters

*Sefer Elim* is the clearest evidence that the scientific revolution was being read
and argued about in Hebrew as it happened, not decades later. That it took the
form of a reply to a Karaite scholar matters too: the correspondence crosses a
sharp communal boundary, and Delmedigo answers the questions on their merits.
The book is a record of a mind formed by both a rabbinic education and an Italian
university, refusing to keep them in separate compartments.

## About this edition

The translated volume reproduces the Odessa 1864 edition; the work was first
printed at Amsterdam in 1629. That printing carries its own apparatus — the
approbations, a statement by the publishers, and a *toledot* or life-history of the
author, styled there as the rabbi, gaon, investigator, kabbalist and divine
philosopher, which conveys how the nineteenth century wished to present him.
