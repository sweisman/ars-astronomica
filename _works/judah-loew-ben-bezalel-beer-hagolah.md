Maharal's defense of the Aggadah — the non-legal portions of the Talmud —
against the critics of his age, who read its stories as absurd, its science as
discredited, and its authors as credulous.

## What is in this book

Written in Prague in 1598 and cast as seven wells sunk toward a single spring,
the book takes up the passages critics found most indefensible and argues that
the Sages were not making the claims their detractors attribute to them. The
recurring move is a claim about register: aggadic statements operate
figuratively and conceptually, and read as literal physical assertions they will of
course appear absurd — but that reading is the critic's error, not the Sages'.

The structure matters to the argument. Each well is a descent toward the same
source, so the book returns repeatedly to the same principle from different
directions rather than advancing through a list of objections.

## Why it matters

The challenge Maharal answers was sharpened by his own century. Where earlier
readers might have found an aggadic passage merely puzzling, sixteenth-century
critics could point to specific astronomical and medical claims in the Talmud that
contemporary science had superseded — and conclude that the Sages had simply
been wrong, and that their authority in other matters fell with it.

That places this book squarely in the corpus's central question. Maharal was in
Prague in the years when David Gans, his younger contemporary, was visiting
Tycho Brahe at Benátky; *Be'er HaGolah* and *Nechmad ve-Na'im* are two
responses to the same pressure, one arguing about how the tradition should be
read, the other bringing the new science into Hebrew directly.
