A complete medieval system of mathematical astronomy, formally Book V, Part 1
of the *Wars of the Lord*. Across 136 chapters it sets out solar and lunar theory,
eclipses, the motions of the planets, and the fixed stars, grounded throughout in
its author's own instruments and observations.

## What is in this book

The manuscript opens with a preface and a chapter list, and the work proper
declares its own scale: divided into one hundred and thirty-six chapters, as
promised. The early chapters build the mathematical ground before any
astronomy is attempted; the middle of the work turns to the models, and the
folios carry not only prose but the apparatus of a practising astronomer —
double-entry tables with cycle-argument columns, argument columns running
along the leaf edges, footer captions beneath the grids, and geometrical diagrams
set into the text.

The codex also preserves the traces of use. Marginalia appear in a second hand in
the inner margins, and notes run down the outer margins of the early folios —
evidence that this was a text people worked through rather than merely owned.

## Why it matters

Gersonides did not take Ptolemy on authority. He built his own instruments — the
cross-staff known later as the Jacob's staff among them — made his own
observations, and where the measurements disagreed with the received models
he changed the models. That is an unusual stance for the fourteenth century, and
it makes *Sefer HaTechunah* one of the most original works of medieval
astronomy in any language.

It is also the least accessible. The work was never printed and is almost entirely
untranslated, surviving only in manuscript — which is why most accounts of
medieval astronomy discuss Gersonides briefly and at second hand.

## About this edition

The translation works from a Sephardic-hand copy made in 1397 (Paris,
Bibliothèque nationale de France, Hébreu 724, ff. 1r–257v), among the earliest
witnesses to the text.
