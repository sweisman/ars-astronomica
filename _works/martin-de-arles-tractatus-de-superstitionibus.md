Martín de Arles y Andosilla (c. 1451–1521) was a professor of theology and
archdeacon of the valley of Aibar in the cathedral of Pamplona. His *Tractatus
de superstitionibus* is a field report on the popular religion of his own
parishes.

## What is in this book

The treatise opens with the rogation processions his cathedral chapter mounted
against drought and hail, among them the custom of plunging an image of Saint
Peter into the river in the hope of forcing the outcome they prayed for. It then catalogues and judges the observances
he had met on visitation: charms and written *schedulae* worn against illness,
lucky and unlucky days, images cast in bronze or wax under particular
constellations, divination by *pythones* and necromancers, herbs gathered with
formulas, and saints' days observed for the wrong reasons. Each practice is
stated as a question and answered from Aquinas, Gerson and the *Decretum*.

## Why it matters

Written around 1510, it is one of the earliest attempts to distinguish licit
devotion from superstition using evidence the author had gathered himself.

## About this edition

The translation is made from the treatise as printed in the composite
demonological volume issued at Frankfurt in 1581, from the Bibliothèque nationale
de France. Two of its companion pieces in that volume, Nicolas Jacquier's
*Flagellum haereticorum fascinariorum* and Thomas Erastus's *De lamiis*, are
translated separately in this series.
