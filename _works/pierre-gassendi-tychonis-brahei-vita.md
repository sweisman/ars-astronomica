A life of the Danish astronomer Tycho Brahe (1546–1601): his fabled observatory
on the island of Hven, the magnificent instruments he built, the campaigns of
observation that remade astronomy, his bold model of the cosmos, and his
turbulent final years in Prague.

## What is in this book

The life runs to six books, and the volume around it is as interesting as the
narrative. An appendix gathers the material that accumulated around Tycho's
death: a funeral oration on his life and death, an elegy on his passing, a formal
eulogy of him as the Cimbrian Atlas and knight, and a piece on Tycho's elixir —
the medical and alchemical side of his work, which later accounts tend to pass
over.

The volume also reproduces primary matter rather than only describing it. It
carries Tycho's own preface to the accurate restoration of the fixed stars of the
eighth sphere, addressed to the Emperor Rudolf II, together with a comparison of
positions of certain fixed stars as variously given in the several catalogues — the
substance of Tycho's claim to have re-founded stellar astronomy on measurement.
Some unpublished pieces and Tycho's poems close it out, with an index to the life.

Three shorter lives — of Copernicus, Peurbach, and Regiomontanus — round out
the book, setting Brahe within the tradition he inherited.

## Why it matters

Gassendi was a first-rate astronomer and philosopher writing barely fifty years
after Tycho's death, with access to people and papers now lost. That makes this a
foundational source for the history of the science rather than a derivative
biography: much of what is repeated about Hven, the instruments and the final
Prague years traces back here. The grouping with Copernicus, Peurbach and
Regiomontanus is itself an argument — a claim about who the moderns' true
predecessors were.
