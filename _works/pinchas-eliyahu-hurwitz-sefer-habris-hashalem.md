A sweeping Hebrew encyclopedia of science and mysticism, among the most
widely read Hebrew books of its era.

## What is in this book

The work is in two parts, and the division is the whole design. The first surveys
the natural sciences of the late Enlightenment — astronomy, physics, geography,
chemistry, biology, and medicine — drawing on contemporary European learning
and presenting it to a Hebrew readership that had, for the most part, no other
route to it.

The second part turns to metaphysics, ethics, the soul, prophecy, and Kabbalah,
integrating the new science with Lurianic mystical thought. Hurwitz does not treat
these as rival accounts to be kept apart; the point of putting them in one book is
that he takes them to be describing one world.

Its chapters on cosmology and on the possibility of extraterrestrial life are among
the earliest Jewish treatments of the subject — a question that only becomes
askable once the plurality of worlds is on the table, and which Hurwitz addresses
with the seriousness of someone who has read the astronomy.

## Why it matters

*Sefer HaBris* was a bestseller in Hebrew, reprinted many times, and for a great
many readers it was where the sciences of the eighteenth century were first
encountered. That gives it an influence out of proportion to its originality: what
Hurwitz chose to include, and how he framed it against the mystical tradition,
shaped what a large Hebrew-reading public understood modern science to be.

It also marks the far end of the arc this corpus traces. Where bar Ḥiyya had to
invent a Hebrew vocabulary for astronomy in the twelfth century, Hurwitz
inherits one and can spend his effort on reconciliation instead.

## About this edition

This volume presents the expanded *HaShalem* ("Complete") edition; the work
was first published in 1797. It is quoted in the front matter of every book in this
corpus — the passage on the duty owed to those who invent useful instruments
and good books.
