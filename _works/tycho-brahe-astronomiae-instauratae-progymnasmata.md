The fullest statement of the observational program that transformed the science
of the heavens. It reconstructs the motions of the Sun and Moon with
unprecedented accuracy, presents the most precise catalog of fixed stars ever
compiled without a telescope, and gives a sweeping account of the brilliant new
star of 1572.

## What is in this book

Three great undertakings sit inside one volume. The solar and lunar theory comes
first, rebuilt from Brahe's own observations rather than inherited tables. The star
catalogue follows — positions measured to a precision no one had reached before
and no one would exceed until the telescope. And then the *stella nova* of 1572,
treated at length: the star that appeared in Cassiopeia, shone brighter than Venus,
and faded away over months.

The nova is the argument the book is remembered for. Like the comet of 1577, it
showed no parallax, which placed it among the fixed stars — proof that even the
supposedly changeless heavens could give birth to something new. Brahe
assembles not only his own observations but a critical survey of what others had
recorded, weighing their instruments and their competence.

## Why it matters

*Progymnasmata* means "preliminary exercises," and the modesty is deliberate:
Brahe intended these as groundwork for a complete restored astronomy he did not
live to write. What survives is nonetheless the most substantial body of accurate
positional data assembled before the telescope, and the direct ancestor of the
Rudolphine Tables.

Its publication history is part of its significance. Left unfinished at Brahe's death, it
was carried to press by Johannes Kepler, who added an appendix of his own —
the same Kepler who would shortly use Brahe's Mars observations to overturn the
system Brahe had spent his life defending.
