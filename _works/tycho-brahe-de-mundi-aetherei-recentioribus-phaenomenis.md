When a brilliant comet blazed across Europe in 1577, the finest instruments of the
age were turned upon it, and the measurements shattered the ancient belief in
unchanging, perfect heavens: the comet moved freely where solid crystalline
spheres were supposed to be.

## What is in this book

The first part follows the comet night by night from November 1577 into January
1578 — a running observational log giving its position, the direction of its tail, and
its changing brightness.

To fix the comet's positions Brahe had first to fix the stars, and the second
chapter works through the reference stars one at a time: the bright star of the
Lyre, the shoulders of Aquarius, the mouth and wing and knee and breast of
Pegasus, Scheat, the bright star of the falling eagle. Each is given a measured
longitude and latitude, tabulated against both the Alphonsine and the Copernican
computations so the reader can see where the older tables fail. An addition by the
author, drawn from later years and better instruments, revises the figures again.

From measurement the book turns to argument. The comet showed no measurable
parallax, placing it far beyond the Moon — in the aethereal region where, on
Aristotelian physics, nothing new could be generated and nothing solid could be
crossed. The later chapters draw the consequence and set out Brahe's own
arrangement of the cosmos: the Earth at rest at the centre, the Sun circling the
Earth, and the remaining planets circling the Sun.

## Why it matters

The dispute Brahe entered was old — whether comets are sublunary weather or
celestial objects — and he settled it with numbers rather than authority. If the
comet passed through the spheres, the spheres were not there. That inference did
more damage to the inherited cosmos than any argument from Copernicus, and it
did so on ground the Aristotelians had chosen themselves.

## About this edition

The work is of 1588, but the translated source is the later Frankfurt printing
issued from the house of Gottfried Tampach, begun at Uraniborg and completed at
Prague. It carries a dedication to the imperial councillor Johannes Barvitius
written not by Brahe but by his son-in-law, who saw the volume through the press
after Brahe's death.
