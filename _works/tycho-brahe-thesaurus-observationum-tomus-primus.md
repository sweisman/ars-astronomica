For twenty-one years on the island of Hven, and afterwards in Bohemia, Tycho
Brahe and his assistants recorded what they saw. The *Thesaurus Observationum*
is that record, gathered from the surviving logbooks by J. L. E. Dreyer for his
edition of Tycho's complete works.

## What is in this book

This first volume opens with Dreyer's *Prolegomena*, his account of the
manuscripts, how they came down, and how he has edited them. Then come the
observations, year by year. They begin in 1563 with the first observation Tycho
is known to have recorded, made at sixteen. They run through his student years
and his time in Augsburg, then through the Hven years up to 1585.

What the logs hold is measurement: positions of the Sun, Moon and planets, the
distances and meridian altitudes of the fixed stars, and right ascensions worked
out by triangulation from the planets. The instruments used are often named
alongside the readings.

## Why it matters

The published books of Tycho Brahe give his conclusions. The logbooks give the
evidence. This is the observational record from which Kepler, working from
Tycho's data, derived the laws of planetary motion. Reading it shows what
astronomical observation was in the last generation before the telescope: how
often it was done, how carefully it was checked, and how much labour lay behind
each position in a table.

## About this edition

The source is volume X of Dreyer's *Tychonis Brahe Dani Opera Omnia*
(Copenhagen, 1923), the first of its volumes of observations. The next volume,
covering 1586 to 1589, is
[*Thesaurus Observationum, Tomus Secundus*](/works/tycho-brahe-thesaurus-observationum-tomus-secundus/).
