The founding text of modern geodesy. In 1615 Snellius measured the arc of the
meridian between Alkmaar and Bergen op Zoom by chaining a network of
triangles across the towns of Holland — the first survey to determine the size of
the Earth by triangulation rather than by pacing or dead reckoning.

## What is in this book

Book I is a critical reconstruction of the ancient measurements, sifting
Eratosthenes, Posidonius, Ptolemy and Strabo against one another. Snellius is not
paying homage: he is establishing what the ancients actually claimed, on what
evidence, and where their figures cannot be reconciled — clearing the ground for
a measurement that will settle the question.

Book II is his own survey. It sets out the instruments, the baselines, the observed
angles and the computations, and arrives at a degree of the meridian of 55,100
toises. The volume also carries a set of carefully recorded latitudes of places, by
which the chorographies of the provinces could be corrected.

The method is the contribution. Rather than measure a long distance directly —
impossible over any useful span — Snellius measures one short baseline with
care, then determines a chain of triangles by angle alone, carrying the known
length across the whole network by trigonometry. The Dutch landscape, flat and
studded with church towers, was almost purpose-built for it.

## Why it matters

The title is a claim: Eratosthenes had measured the Earth in antiquity, and
Snellius means to do it again properly, as a Dutchman. He succeeded well enough
that the technique he introduced governed geodesy until the satellite era —
national surveys, map projections and the figure of the Earth all rest on
triangulation for the next three centuries.

It also belongs beside the astronomy in this corpus for a practical reason: the size
of the Earth is the baseline against which every astronomical distance is
eventually measured.
