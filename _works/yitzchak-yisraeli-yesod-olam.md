One of the great medieval Hebrew treatises on astronomy and the science of the
Jewish calendar, composed in Toledo in the first half of the fourteenth century.

## What is in this book

The work builds a complete system step by step, and the order is the argument:
nothing is used before it has been established. It begins with geometry and
trigonometry, rises through a model of the celestial spheres and the motions of
Sun and Moon, and culminates in the rules of the Hebrew calendar together with a
set of astronomical tables.

That progression makes *Yesod Olam* the fullest answer in the corpus to a
question its other calendrical works raise but do not always pursue: not merely
how the calendar is computed, but why those computations are the right ones —
what model of the heavens they presuppose, and what geometry that model in
turn requires. The manuscript opens with the author's verse preface.

## Why it matters

Toledo in this period was where Arabic, Latin and Hebrew learning met, and
Yisraeli writes as an inheritor of all three. The book became a standard reference
for later Hebrew writers on the calendar, and it is the natural companion to bar
Ḥiyya's *Sefer HaIbbur* two centuries earlier — where bar Ḥiyya established the
discipline, Yisraeli gives it its most complete medieval statement.

## About this edition

This witness is the richly preserved 1484 copy in Bodleian MS. Huntington 299,
chosen as a cleaner and higher-resolution alternative to the Vatican copy. The
translation follows the manuscript from its first text leaf, and the volume
preserves the codex's front matter — binding, pastedown and flyleaves — before
the text begins.
