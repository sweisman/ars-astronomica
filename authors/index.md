# Authors

The 35 authors whose works are in the corpus. [Browse by work instead](/works/).

- **[Athanasius Kircher](/authors/athanasius-kircher/)** (1602–1680). German Jesuit polymath in Rome whose illustrated folios ranged from magnetism and music to Egypt, China and the interior of the Earth. 1 work.
- **[R. Avraham bar Ḥiyya ha-Nasi](/authors/avraham-bar-hiyya-ha-nasi/)** (c. 1070–c. 1136). Mathematician and astronomer of Barcelona, among the first to write science in Hebrew rather than Arabic. 3 works.
- **[R. Avraham ibn Ezra](/authors/avraham-ibn-ezra/)** (c. 1089–c. 1167). Poet, biblical commentator and astronomer from Tudela, who carried the science of Muslim Spain to the Jews of Italy, France and England. 1 work.
- **[Bartolomeo Spina](/authors/bartolomeo-spina/)** (c. 1474–1546). Dominican theologian from Pisa, later Master of the Sacred Palace, who argued that the witches' flight was real. 1 work.
- **[Chaim Vital](/authors/chaim-vital/)** (1542–1620). Kabbalist of Safed and Damascus, foremost disciple of Isaac Luria, who also wrote a treatise on the astronomy of the spheres. 1 work.
- **[Christoph Clavius](/authors/christoph-clavius/)** (1538–1612). Jesuit mathematician at the Collegio Romano, architect of the Gregorian calendar and teacher of a generation of astronomers. 5 works.
- **[Christoph Scheiner](/authors/christoph-scheiner/)** (1573–1650). Jesuit astronomer who observed sunspots for two decades, showed that the Sun rotates, and disputed the discovery with Galileo. 2 works.
- **[David Gans](/authors/david-gans/)** (1541–1613). Prague chronicler and astronomer, a student of the Maharal, who visited Tycho Brahe's observatory and described what he saw. 2 works.
- **[David Origanus](/authors/david-origanus/)** (1558–1628). Professor of Greek and mathematics at Frankfurt an der Oder whose ephemerides were computed on both Tychonic and Copernican foundations. 1 work.
- **[R. Eliyahu Mizrahi](/authors/eliyahu-mizrahi/)** (c. 1455–1526). Chief rabbi of the Ottoman Empire, author of the standard supercommentary on Rashi, and one of the leading Jewish mathematicians of his age. 1 work.
- **[Ernesto Bozzano](/authors/ernesto-bozzano/)** (1862–1943). Italian psychical researcher of Genoa who spent fifty years collecting and classifying reported cases. 1 work.
- **[Giovanni Battista Riccioli](/authors/giovanni-battista-riccioli/)** (1598–1671). Jesuit astronomer of Bologna whose New Almagest weighed every argument about the Earth's motion and named the craters of the Moon. 3 works.
- **[Govaert Wendelen](/authors/govaert-wendelen/)** (1580–1667). Flemish priest and astronomer, a careful observer who corresponded with Riccioli, Gassendi and Mersenne. 3 works.
- **[Helisaeus Roeslin](/authors/helisaeus-roeslin/)** (1545–1616). Physician and astrologer in Alsace who set the rival systems of the world side by side in 1597 and added a geo-heliocentric one of his own. 1 work.
- **[R. Immanuel Bonfils](/authors/immanuel-bonfils/)** (c. 1300–1377). Astronomer of Tarascon in Provence whose Six Wings were used across the Jewish world and translated into Latin and Greek. 1 work.
- **[R. Isaac ibn al-Aḥdab](/authors/isaac-ibn-al-ahdab/)** (c. 1350–c. 1430). Castilian-born astronomer, mathematician and poet who settled in Sicily, where he compiled tables for the Sun and Moon. 2 works.
- **[R. Jacob ben Machir ibn Tibbon](/authors/jacob-ben-machir-ibn-tibbon/)** (c. 1236–c. 1305). Astronomer and translator of Montpellier, known in Latin as Profatius, who invented the new quadrant. 1 work.
- **[Jacob ben Samson](/authors/jacob-ben-samson/)**. A pupil of Rashi in northern France, to whom an early Ashkenazi commentary on the Hebrew calendar is attributed. 1 work.
- **[Johannes Hevelius](/authors/johannes-hevelius/)** (1611–1687). Danzig brewer and councillor who built Europe's largest observatory on his rooftops and mapped the Moon. 3 works.
- **[Joseph Solomon Delmedigo](/authors/joseph-solomon-delmedigo/)** (1591–1655). Physician from Crete who studied under Galileo at Padua and wrote one of the most ambitious Hebrew scientific books of his age. 1 work.
- **[R. Judah ben Solomon ha-Kohen](/authors/judah-ben-solomon-ha-kohen/)** (c. 1215–c. 1274). Toledan scholar who corresponded with the court of Frederick II and wrote one of the first scientific encyclopedias in Hebrew. 1 work.
- **[R. Judah Loew ben Bezalel (Maharal)](/authors/judah-loew-ben-bezalel/)** (d. 1609). Rabbi of Prague, known as the Maharal, and one of the most original Jewish thinkers of the sixteenth century. 1 work.
- **[R. Levi ben Gershom (Gersonides)](/authors/levi-ben-gershom/)** (1288–1344). Philosopher, mathematician and astronomer of Provence, known as Gersonides, who tested Ptolemy against his own observations. 2 works.
- **[Longomontanus](/authors/longomontanus/)** (1562–1647). Tycho Brahe's principal assistant, later professor at Copenhagen, who wrote the full account of the Tychonic system. 1 work.
- **[Martín de Arles](/authors/martin-de-arles/)** (c. 1451–1521). Navarrese theologian and archdeacon who recorded, and judged, the popular beliefs of his own parishes. 1 work.
- **[R. Mordecai Comtino](/authors/mordecai-comtino/)** (15th century). Rabbanite scholar of Constantinople who taught mathematics and astronomy to Rabbanites and Karaites alike. 1 work.
- **[Nicolas Jacquier](/authors/nicolas-jacquier/)** (d. 1472). Dominican inquisitor who wrote the first sustained defence of the reality of the witches' sabbath. 1 work.
- **[Nicolaus Reimers Ursus](/authors/nicolaus-reimers-ursus/)** (1551–1600). Self-taught mathematician from Dithmarschen, imperial mathematician to Rudolf II, and Tycho Brahe's bitterest rival. 1 work.
- **[Pedro Nunes](/authors/pedro-nunes/)** (1502–1578). Royal cosmographer of Portugal and the leading mathematician of the age of discoveries, who founded the mathematics of navigation. 1 work.
- **[Pierre Gassendi](/authors/pierre-gassendi/)** (1592–1655). French priest, philosopher and astronomer who revived the atomism of Epicurus and was the first to observe a transit of Mercury. 2 works.
- **[R. Pinchas Eliyahu Hurwitz](/authors/pinchas-eliyahu-hurwitz/)** (1765–1821). Kabbalist from Vilna whose Sefer HaBris brought Enlightenment science to traditional Hebrew readers across Europe. 1 work.
- **[Thomas Erastus](/authors/thomas-erastus/)** (1524–1583). Swiss physician and theologian, professor of medicine at Heidelberg, opponent of Paracelsus, and the source of the word Erastianism. 1 work.
- **[Tycho Brahe](/authors/tycho-brahe/)** (1546–1601). Danish nobleman whose island observatory produced the most accurate observations made before the telescope. 9 works.
- **[Willebrord Snellius](/authors/willebrord-snellius/)** (1580–1626). Leiden professor of mathematics who measured the Earth by triangulation and found the law of refraction. 2 works.
- **[R. Yitzchak Yisraeli](/authors/yitzchak-yisraeli/)** (fl. c. 1310). Astronomer of Toledo and student of the Rosh, whose Yesod Olam grounds the Hebrew calendar in mathematical astronomy. 1 work.
